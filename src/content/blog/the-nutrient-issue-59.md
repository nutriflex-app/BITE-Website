On November 4th, a modeling study published in *Nature Health* put a number on something nutrition scientists have argued for years: that most of the world's table salt problem isn't really a salt problem, it's a sodium-versus-potassium problem. The researchers modeled what would happen across 193 countries if people swapped ordinary sodium chloride salt for a potassium-enriched blend, the kind that replaces a quarter to a third of the sodium with potassium chloride and tastes close enough to regular salt that most people don't notice. Their projection: close to three million deaths prevented worldwide every year, plus roughly ten million fewer cases of cardiovascular and kidney disease, if the swap were made broadly. Even a narrower version of it, just getting 80% of people with diagnosed high blood pressure to switch their home salt, was projected to prevent about 590,000 deaths a year worldwide and roughly 105,000 deaths and 492,000 new cases of heart and kidney disease a year in the US alone. The model leans on real trial evidence, not just theory: China's Salt Substitute and Stroke Study followed almost 21,000 rural adults for five years and found the swap cut stroke risk 14%, major cardiovascular events 13%, and death from any cause 12%, compared with regular salt.

The study is also careful about who it isn't for. Potassium-enriched salt is risky for anyone whose kidneys can't clear potassium normally, since the whole mechanism that makes it protective for most people, more potassium in the blood, can push someone with impaired kidney function into dangerously high potassium levels instead. The World Health Organization's own guidance says people with kidney conditions that impair potassium removal should avoid potassium-enriched salt substitutes unless a healthcare professional tells them otherwise. It's the one dietary swap in recent memory where the size of the benefit and the sharpness of the exception are both unusually well quantified.

That exception is also, word for word, a condition Mr Bite's own code already treats as serious enough to act on. `mrBiteSafety.ts`, in `supabase/functions/_shared/`, defines `MEDICAL_HARD_BLOCK_RE`, the regex that decides when the coaching chat has to stop giving nutrition advice and redirect someone to a clinician instead. Spelled out in the pattern, among diagnosis, treatment, and medication: `kidney disease` and `renal disease`. If a user so much as mentions either phrase, Mr Bite's own safety layer treats it as a hard stop, the same tier as chest pain or a medical emergency. The app has clearly made a deliberate decision that kidney status changes what nutrition advice is safe to give.

So I went looking for what Mr Bite actually knows about potassium, the one nutrient that decision is really about, and the answer is nothing. I ran a case-insensitive search for "potassium" across the entire repository, every screen, every function, every migration. Zero files matched. Not a type, not a comment, not a column name. Compare that to sodium, which the app tracks closely: `NutritionLabelProps`, the interface behind `NutritionLabel.tsx`, lists fourteen named nutrients it knows how to render, calories, fat, saturated fat, trans fat, cholesterol, sodium, carbs, fiber, sugars, protein, vitamin A, vitamin C, calcium, and iron. Potassium isn't the fifteenth. There's no slot for it to go in.

The gap starts even earlier, at the point where recipe data enters the app. `spoonacularMapper.ts`, in `src/utils/`, takes the nutrition object Spoonacular's API hands back for a recipe and picks out specific nutrients by name: `getNutrientAmount(input.nutrition, ['Sodium'])`, and the same pattern for fiber, sugar, saturated fat, vitamin C, iron, and calcium. Each of those is a deliberate, named lookup into a list of nutrients the API already returns. Sodium made that list. Potassium was simply never one of the names the mapper was told to look for, so whatever the data held, nothing was ever built to catch it.

I want to be fair about what this isn't. Mr Bite doesn't sell salt and isn't in a position to tell anyone to switch brands, and the *Nature Health* paper is a model, not a clinical trial, with all the caveats that come with modeling 193 countries' worth of eating habits at once. But the app's own safety code has already decided that kidney disease is a condition worth hard-blocking coaching over. It just built that rule without ever building the one data field that would let it explain why.

1. **Family Farmstead Dairy recalled all of its raw mild and raw sharp cheddar cheese around November 10th to 12th after testing raised concerns about Listeria monocytogenes contamination.** The 8-ounce, plastic-wrapped wheels were sold in 12 Northeast and Mid-Atlantic states with lot codes dated October 16th, 2027 and earlier; it follows an October recall from the same company over possible E. coli contamination in a separate raw cheddar batch, and no illnesses have been reported from the Listeria concern. [KBTX](https://www.kbtx.com/2026/11/12/listeria-concerns-prompt-family-farmstead-dairy-cheddar-cheese-recall/)

2. **A Columbia University-led review published November 1st in the *Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs* combined more than 7,200 existing studies with US health data and found no level of drinking that comes out protective once every alcohol-linked disease and injury is counted together.** At around two drinks a day, lifetime alcohol-attributable mortality reached roughly 1 in 25; the review did find small reductions in heart disease and stroke at low intakes, but concluded they were outweighed by increases in cancer and other harms. [Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/news/new-research-links-even-low-alcohol-consumption-cancer-heart-disease-premature-death)

3. **The FDA published a proposed rule on November 2nd for its first comprehensive review of infant formula nutrient requirements since 1998, under Docket No. FDA-2026-N-10832.** Of the 30 nutrients currently required in formula, the agency says it evaluated eight and is proposing changes to four while leaving the rest as is; public comments are open through March 2nd, 2027, and the agency separately launched a new Infant Formula Chemical Contaminants Dashboard alongside the rule. [HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/press-room/fda-updates-infant-formula-standards-first-time-30-years.html)

## Tool of the Week: USDA's FoodData Central

![A laptop screen showing a simple dinner plate icon with a small magnifying glass over it and a checkmark icon beside it, a government-building silhouette faint in the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261125_081639_393bc201-36ab-4b2a-b6f6-41d1cfde8476.png)

Since this issue is about a nutrient Mr Bite's own pipeline has never once looked for, the pick this week is the free government database that's been tracking it the whole time. USDA's FoodData Central lets anyone search any food, branded or generic, and see its full nutrient breakdown, potassium included, down to the milligram, sourced from the same kind of lab analysis the government uses to set its own dietary guidance. It won't warn a specific user away from a specific product the way an app in their pocket could, but it's the clearest free way to find out exactly how much potassium is sitting in tonight's dinner, something Mr Bite's own nutrition label still can't tell you. [USDA's FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/)
