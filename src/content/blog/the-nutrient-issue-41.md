This week FoodNavigator and Food Safety magazine both picked back up an analysis commissioned by the International Food Information Council and run by NIQ, looking at more than 40,000 US grocery products across five categories households with kids buy constantly: cereal, snacks, fresh dairy, prepared meals, and fruits and vegetables. The question was simple: if you already know a product is "ultra-processed" under the NOVA system, how much does that actually tell you about its nutrient quality? The answer, scored against the Nutrient Rich Food Index 6.3, is: less than the label implies. Non-UPFs came out ahead on average, but the spread inside every single category was wide enough that some of the most processed products on shelf beat plenty of "whole" ones on nutrient density, and the ultra-processed products were consistently the cheaper option and the ones actually filling most of the cart. IFIC is an industry-funded group, so read its framing with that in mind, but the core number is hard to argue with: a NOVA group alone is a blunt instrument, and a single bucket (1 through 4) can't tell you what's actually in the thing.

That's a specific, falsifiable claim about the danger of collapsing real nutrient variation into one number. So I went looking for every place Mr Bite already does exactly that, and I found one, sitting in plain sight on the recipe screens.

It's called, honestly, `healthScore`, and it isn't ours. It comes straight from Spoonacular, the third-party API that backs our recipe library, and we never calculated it ourselves. `RecipeCard.tsx` renders it as a small colored badge in the corner of a recipe photo whenever `healthScore` and `healthScoreColor` are both present. `RecipePreviewScreen.tsx` does more with it: inside a block gated on `recipe.is_spoonacular && !isEditing`, it reads `recipe.spoonacular_health_score`, picks a color (green at 70 and up, amber from 40 to 69, red below 40), and renders a ring with the raw number inside it next to a one-line verdict: "Excellent choice," "Good choice," or "Could be healthier." The same block tacks on diet pills, Vegan, Gluten-Free, Very Healthy, Sustainable, Budget Friendly, straight from fields named `spoonacular_vegan`, `spoonacular_very_healthy`, `spoonacular_sustainable`, and a `spoonacular_price_per_serving` cutoff. Every one of those is Spoonacular's own number or flag, carried through unmodified. I grepped the whole codebase for "NOVA," "ultra-processed," "UPF," "nutrient density," and "NRF." Zero hits, outside this newsletter's own drafts. We don't compute a quality score. We display someone else's.

I don't think that's a scandal by itself, buying a third-party signal instead of building one is a completely reasonable call. What bothers me is what it does once a user actually sees it. "Excellent choice" and "Could be healthier" read like Mr Bite's own verdict on a recipe, in Mr Bite's own UI, with no disclosure that the number underneath came from somewhere else or what it's actually weighing. Nobody on our team could tell you, off the top of their head, why a given recipe scored 42 instead of 68. That's precisely the failure mode this week's report is warning about: a single number standing in for real, multi-dimensional nutrient content, minus even the virtue of being transparent about its own formula.

And it's inconsistent on top of being opaque. The whole block is gated on `recipe.is_spoonacular`. A recipe a user builds themselves, a meal they log by hand, a product they scan at the store, none of those ever get a health score, good or bad, because none of them come from Spoonacular. A user can log the exact same ingredients two different ways and get a verdict for one and silence for the other, with no way to know that's why.

The honest fix isn't to build our own competing 0-to-100 score; this week's report is a good argument against trusting any single number that much, ours included. It's smaller than that: either label the badge as Spoonacular's own assessment so a user knows what they're looking at, or stop surfacing an unexplained verdict we can't ourselves account for. Right now we're doing neither, on about half the recipes in the app, by accident.

Michael shipped a real retention fix to main on October 19th. The data behind it: in the three weeks through October 17th, the fast-tracker paths, barcode, search, manual entry, carried over 1,100 logs between them, while the average visit to the Mr Bite chat tab lasted 9 seconds, and people who opened chat on day one were more likely to churn than people who didn't. So new accounts no longer land on the chat tab after the onboarding quiz. They land on Home with the quick-add dock already open: search, recent, photo, scan, voice, manual, all one tap away. The targets card, which used to be hidden behind the old chat landing, now shows up right after a user's first logged meal instead. The chat-based welcome still exists, but it's been rewritten to follow the first log rather than precede it: instead of asking a brand-new user what they last ate, it now reacts to what they already logged ("Chicken salad is in. Nice start."), and the onboarding checklist drops the redundant "log a meal" task entirely. It's a JS-only change, no native code touched, so it's OTA-eligible the moment it ships; the build in app stores is still the October 13th 1.17.0 release, so none of this is live for users yet.

## Three Quick Bites

![Three vignettes in a row: a pint container of gelato beside a glowing bacterium icon, a seed packet beside a magnifying glass over a small sprouting seedling, and a shopping basket beside a small injection pen icon and a protein shake bottle, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261021_081707_540092e4-87e7-4ab9-a5ec-62b885c84bfc.png)

1. **Lidl US recalled one lot of Gelatelli Coffee & Vanilla Gelato with Coffee Sauce & Cookie Pieces on October 17th after routine testing turned up Listeria monocytogenes.** The affected pints (UPC 4 0564897 22458, lot 085261, best-by March 26, 2028) went to Lidl stores across Delaware, DC, Georgia, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, and Virginia between July 19th and October 15th. No illnesses have been reported; buyers are told to toss it or return it for a refund. [Fox13 News](https://www.fox13news.com/news/gelato-sold-lidl-stores-multiple-states-recalled-over-possible-listeria-contamination)

2. **EAK Distribution recalled its HOME GROWN Green Sprouting Calabrese Broccoli Seeds (8 oz, heirloom) on October 2nd after FDA testing found Salmonella in a seed sample.** The seeds sold on Amazon nationwide from August 20th to September 18th, and EAK says it was first flagged by its own seed supplier on September 16th, a full twelve days before the FDA's own result came back. No illnesses have been reported, but the seed lot traces to a grower caught up in a broader federal sprout investigation, worth knowing if you grow your own. [Oregon Department of Agriculture](https://apps.oregon.gov/oregon-newsroom/OR/FS/Posts/Post/EAK-Distribution-LLC-RECALLS-HOME-GROWN-Green-Sprouting-Calabrese-Broccoli-Seeds-8oz-Heirloom-BECAUSE-OF-POSSIBLE-HEALTH-RISK-PR)

3. **A National Frozen & Refrigerated Foods Association survey, recirculated by trade press on October 16th, found that over half of GLP-1 users (52 percent) report buying more high-protein dairy, and 48 percent more single-serve, portion-controlled foods.** Protein drinks were up for 43 percent, frozen proteins for 42 percent. The underlying survey ran with Morning Consult back in June across 2,003 adults, 241 of them on a GLP-1, so the subgroup is small, but the direction lines up with what every grocery chain is now reporting: feeling full and eating enough protein have become the two things this cohort shops for first. [Perishable News](https://perishablenews.com/retailfoodservice/new-research-glp-1-users-reshaping-the-grocery-basket/)

## Tool of the Week: Yuka

![A hand holding a phone with a barcode scan beam crossing a packaged product, beside a small scorecard showing a round number dial and three small colored dots in a row representing risk levels, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261021_081710_cba98de0-3f21-47f6-9b0b-914a54935540.png)

Since this issue is about a health score nobody can explain, the pick this week is a free barcode scanner that actually publishes its formula. Yuka scores packaged food 0 to 100 from three named, weighted parts: 60 percent Nutri-Score, 30 percent additive risk (each additive gets its own color-coded risk level), and 10 percent whether it's organic, with a hard cap at 49 if there's a high-risk additive or a D/E Nutri-Score underneath. It isn't perfect, critics fairly point out it can overweight additives relative to the overall diet, but a user can go look up exactly why a product scored what it scored. That's the bar. A number without a reachable "why" isn't a health score, it's a mood. [Yuka](https://yuka.io/en/)
