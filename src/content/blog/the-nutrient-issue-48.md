On August 3rd, HHS and the FDA sent the White House's Office of Management and Budget a proposed federal definition of "ultra-processed food," the first the U.S. government has ever tried to write. It followed a request for public comment that opened in July 2025 and drew more than 5,000 responses. Since then, the definition has sat at OMB with no public details and no release date. By late October, more than 70 public health researchers, dietitians, and advocacy groups were pushing the budget office to publish it, with a meeting planned for October 23rd. The Center for Science in the Public Interest's deputy director told Newsweek it "remains stuck" there, with no timetable. FDA's own food chief said back in July that writing the definition had proven "quite challenging," since multiple competing definitions, built around different research traditions, are already in use.

That's a genuinely hard problem for a federal agency, because "ultra-processed" isn't one ingredient or one number, it's a classification system, and the leading one, NOVA, sorts food into four groups by how it's made, not what's in it. A tracking app doesn't have to solve the definitional fight to be useful here. It just has to show someone what the rest of the world has already classified about the box in their hand. So I went looking for whether Mr Bite does that, for packaged food scanned from a barcode, which is most of what actually gets logged.

It doesn't, because the concept doesn't exist anywhere in this codebase. A repo-wide, case-insensitive search for "nova" or "ultra-processed" across every file, every test, every migration, returns nothing. `PackagedFood`, the type every barcode scan and every search result gets normalized into, defined in `src/types/index.ts`, carries exactly six nutrients: calories, protein, carbs, fats, fiber, sugar. Nothing else. No processing tier, no additive count, no Nutri-Score. That part isn't a surprise on its own, plenty of nutrition apps only ever track macros. What makes it worth writing about is where the data actually comes from.

`lookupOpenFoodFactsByBarcode` in `openFoodFactsService.ts` calls Open Food Facts' `/api/v2/product/{barcode}.json` endpoint with no `fields` parameter at all, which means it gets back the full product document Open Food Facts has on file, every time someone scans something. That document already carries `nova_group`, a 1-to-4 processing-tier score volunteer editors have been attaching to Open Food Facts entries for years, the exact kind of classification the federal government is still struggling to define from scratch. `normalizeOffProduct`, the function that turns that raw response into a `PackagedFood`, reads `product.nutriments`, `product.product_name`, `product.brands`, `product.categories_tags`, `product.serving_size`, and the image fields. It never reads `product.nova_group`. The number arrives in the response and is thrown away before the function returns, on every single scan, the same pattern Issue 46 found with potassium: the data shows up for free, and nothing downstream looks at it.

The two places in the app that are supposed to narrow search results, `OFF_SEARCH_V2_FIELDS` and `OFF_SEARCH_CGI_FIELDS`, list out every field they want by name, twenty-some of them, for product names in five languages, brand strings, serving sizes. Neither list includes `nova_group` either, so even the narrower search path was never going to carry it. Someone would have had to decide not to ask for it, twice, in two different field lists, and then decide not to read it a third time in the barcode path where it arrives whether anyone asked or not.

None of this means Mr Bite should wait on Washington to figure out its own definition before doing anything. Open Food Facts has been publishing NOVA groups on millions of products for years, under a license that's free to use, and the federal definition, whenever it lands, is going to answer a narrower question than "is this processed" anyway, since OMB's review is reportedly not shaping up as a binding rule. The irony is just that the one piece of this fight an app like ours could ship this month, unconditionally, without an opinion on anyone's politics, is sitting in a field we already pull down and currently delete.

## BITE of the Week

![A single chat bubble containing one small receipt checkmark icon, surrounded by many faint ghost-outline duplicates of the same icon fading away into the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261103_081521_30e228cc-3c1e-4838-8bf6-6748c7a59f16.png)

A bug fix this time, and a specific one. Mr Bite can plan out a user's week in one chat turn, saving a batch of meals in the process, and until this week, a big plan landed badly: ask him to plan 18 meals and you'd get 18 identical "Planned meal" chips stacked on top of each other, followed by a closer that admitted it "had to stop partway," even on turns where it hadn't. The fix, landed November 2nd, collapses repeated per-meal receipts into one chip, so that same 18-meal plan now shows a single line: "Planned 18 meals, 3 to 8 Nov." The loop-exhaustion closer was also rewritten to report what it actually wrote before falling back to a preview or a give-up line, instead of defaulting to the give-up line out of habit.

It's merged to `main` and part of version 1.18.1, bumped the same day. But "merged" and "live" aren't the same thing here: the OTA runtime stayed at 1.0.6 because this change is JS-only, so it's eligible for an over-the-air push without an app store release, and EAS has assigned build numbers (iOS 122, Android 152) for the native build. Whether you've actually gotten the new chip yet depends on two things nobody but us controls yet: an OTA publish, and a founder-run deploy of the `mr-bite-chat` server function this fix also touches. If your plan replies still look repetitive, that's why.

## Three Quick Bites

![Three vignettes in a row: a USDA inspection seal stamp beside a strip of beef jerky, a bagged chopped salad kit beside a small warning triangle icon, and a wedge of cheese beside a small insect silhouette icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261103_081523_0e1916d6-16b6-4a9a-ab47-c9fdc61582c5.png)

1. **Imperio Distribution LLC of Elgin, Illinois recalled roughly 946 pounds of ready-to-eat beef jerky on October 24th after USDA inspectors found it was made without federal inspection and carried another facility's inspection mark.** The three "Carne Seca" varieties, Fuego, Original, and Limón, were packaged between January and October 2026 and sold in at least seven Midwest states; no illnesses have been reported. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/10/beef-jerky-recalled-because-of-lack-of-inspection-illegal-use-of-usda-seal/)

2. **Taylor Farms recalled H-E-B Backyard BBQ Chop Salad kits on October 27th because they may contain undeclared soy and almonds.** The 13.4-ounce kits, sold only at Texas H-E-B stores with a best-if-used-by date of October 30th, pose a serious risk to anyone with a soy or tree nut allergy; no adverse reactions have been reported so far. [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/taylor-farms-issues-recall-h-e-b-backyard-bbq-chop-salad-kit-due-potential-undeclared-soy-and-tree)

3. **The FDA sent warning letters to a Florida cheese manufacturer and a Minnesota seafood importer this month after inspectors found recurring sanitation failures, including cockroaches on cheese-making equipment and flies around ready-to-eat product at the Florida plant.** Both letters describe problems inspectors had already flagged before, not first-time findings; the companies have 15 days to respond before FDA can pursue further action. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/10/fda-finds-cockroaches-on-cheese-equipment-flies-on-ready-to-eat-products-at-florida-plant/)

## Tool of the Week: RegInfo.gov

![An open government document icon with a small magnifying glass hovering over it, connecting by a simple line out to a plain phone app icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261103_081525_6f534ef7-7b13-4142-bf83-76c6850bf142.png)

Since this issue is about a rule that's been sitting unseen at a federal office for three months, the pick this week is the free government site that actually tracks that: RegInfo.gov, the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs' own public database of every rule under executive review, searchable by agency, and showing the date it arrived at OMB and how long it's been there. It won't tell you what the ultra-processed food definition says, nobody outside the review can see that, but it's the honest, verifiable answer to "is this still stuck," which beats guessing from secondhand reporting. [RegInfo.gov](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eoReviewSearch)
