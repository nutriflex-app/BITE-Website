On November 12th, a study authored by three researchers in the CDC's Division of Nutrition, Physical Activity, and Obesity went up in the journal *Nutrients*, and it did something most added-sugar research doesn't bother with: it tied how closely someone reads a nutrition label to how much added sugar they're actually eating. Using the government's Flexible Consumer Behavior Survey, Koyama, Pejavara, and Self split US adults into frequent and infrequent label checkers and measured each group's added sugar intake separately. Frequent checkers ate about 61.7 grams of added sugar a day. Infrequent checkers ate about 104.4 grams. That's a gap of roughly 43 grams, close to a full can of soda's worth, between two groups of adults eating off the same American food supply, with the measured difference being whether they bothered reading the label before buying it. A smaller version of the same gap showed up in kids, 64.5 grams a day in frequent-label households versus 79.0 in infrequent ones, and among children in the highest-income households specifically, that gap widened back out to about 43 grams, nearly matching the adult number. Across every adult surveyed, average added sugar intake came to 70.3 grams a day, 13.2% of calories, well past the under-10%-of-calories limit the Dietary Guidelines for Americans has used for years.

The study is observational, so it can't say reading labels causes anyone to eat less sugar; label checkers plausibly differ from non-checkers in a dozen ways a survey can't fully untangle. But the number the whole study is built around, added sugar specifically, as opposed to total sugar, only exists because the US Nutrition Facts label makes food companies calculate and print it, right under the regular sugar line, so a shopper doesn't have to guess how much of the sugar in a yogurt came from the fruit versus the spoonful stirred in afterward. Mr Bite builds its own version of that label for every food it shows a user, so I went looking for the "added" half of that line and found it was never built.

`NutritionLabelProps`, the interface behind `NutritionLabel.tsx`, lists fourteen fields: servingSize, servings, calories, totalFat, saturatedFat, transFat, cholesterol, sodium, totalCarbs, fiber, sugars, protein, vitaminA, vitaminC, calcium, iron. One of those, `sugars`, gets rendered with `renderRow('Total Sugars', sugars, 'g', false, true)`, the word "Total" written directly into the label string. There's no `addedSugars` prop sitting next to it, no second row underneath, nothing a developer could switch on later if the data ever showed up. This isn't a missing value. It's a missing field.

It's missing upstream, too. `PackagedFood` and `ServingSize`, both in `types/index.ts`, carry the same six-nutrient `nutrients` object: calories, protein, carbs, fats, fiber, sugar, and `sugar` there means total sugar as well. The two Supabase migrations that touch the word at all, `20251112120000_new_features.sql` and `20260709000000_coach_brain_foundation.sql`, each declare exactly one column, `sugar DECIMAL(10, 2) DEFAULT 0`, with no sibling column for anything added. `spoonacularMapper.ts` asks Spoonacular's API for precisely two nutrient names when it wants this value, `'Sugar'` and `'Sugars'`, and stops there. `openFoodFactsService.ts` does the same against Open Food Facts, pulling `sugars_100g`, `sugars_serving`, and the legacy `sugars` key through a helper called `macroPer100PreferServing`, and nothing else. The barcode lookup that feeds that helper, `lookupOpenFoodFactsByBarcode`, hits `world.openfoodfacts.org/api/v2/product/{barcode}.json` with no `fields` parameter at all, the same gap Issue 48 found in this exact function. The full product document comes back on every single scan, which is how Open Food Facts' own product pages are able to print an "Added sugars" line directly under "Sugars" for plenty of branded foods when a manufacturer has reported it. Mr Bite receives that same document every time and simply never reads for that key.

The coaching chat, meanwhile, already treats "sugar" as a word worth taking seriously. It's one of roughly forty terms in `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE`, the regex in `mr-bite-chat/index.ts` that decides a message needs a careful, sourced answer instead of a casual one, and `mrBiteHealthClaimSources.ts` maps any question containing "sugar," "carbs," or "glycemic" straight to a real citation, MedlinePlus's page on carbohydrates. Ask Mr Bite about added sugar and it'll answer carefully, with a source attached. It just can't tell you the number for what's actually in your bowl, because across the other thirteen files in this codebase that mention the word "sugar," none of them ever separates "added" from the rest. The phrase does turn up once, verbatim, in a unit test: a fake coach reply reading "Added sugars are the real issue in that bar, not the fat," part of a test asserting that the coach hadn't falsely claimed to log something. Even there, the words only show up as something the coach might say out loud, never as something it could look up.

I want to be as fair to the limits of the CDC's own study as its authors would be. It can't prove that printing a number on a label changes what anyone eats, and label-checkers are plausibly just more health-conscious across the board, which could produce the same 43-gram gap whether or not a single added-sugar line existed anywhere. But that's an argument about whether the label is doing the work, not about whether the number is worth having. Mr Bite already decided sugar deserves a sourced, careful answer the moment a user types the word. It just never decided the number was worth keeping.

## Three Quick Bites

1. **ALDI recalled two Priano Protein Tortelloni varieties, Carbonara and Herb & Cheese, around November 16th to 17th after determining the 9-ounce packages may not have been held at safe temperatures, raising a risk of Clostridium botulinum.** Both share lot code T9101926 and a best-by date of December 18th, 2026, and were sold only at select stores in Alabama, Georgia, North Carolina, and South Carolina; no illnesses have been reported, and shoppers are told to discard the product or return it for a refund. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/aldi-recalls-tortelloni-because-of-botulism-risk/)

2. **Fresh & Ready Foods expanded its Listeria recall of Fresh To You biscuit sandwiches on November 17th, adding a second product, Mmm! Meals Made in Minutes Biscuit & Sausage Gravy, after the contamination was traced to a biscuit supplier, C.H. Guenther & Son.** The widened recall reached several more states beyond the original Oregon and Washington release and covers sandwiches sold through vending machines, break rooms, and micro-markets; no illnesses have been reported. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/biscuit-sandwiches-recalled-because-of-listeria-risk/)

3. **Novo Nordisk's STEP Young trial, presented at ObesityWeek and published November 16th in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, found that semaglutide cut BMI by 13.5% over 68 weeks in children ages 6 to 11 with obesity, versus a 2.7% rise in the placebo group.** Seventy-nine percent of children on semaglutide moved down at least one BMI category, versus 23% on placebo, and researchers reported no new safety signals for growth or pubertal development; semaglutide still isn't approved in the US for children under 12, and the trial ran as a post-marketing commitment tied to the drug's existing adult and adolescent approval. [Contemporary Pediatrics](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/semaglutide-cuts-bmi-13-5-in-children-aged-6-to-11-with-obesity)

## Tool of the Week: FDA's Interactive Nutrition Facts Label

![A laptop screen showing a simple nutrition facts label icon with a magnifying glass over its sugar line and a small checkmark icon beside it, a government-building silhouette faint in the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261127_081625_5a0b09d3-6f0b-4aee-958c-ab4c3d28edbe.png)

Since this issue is about a line Mr Bite's own label never grew, the pick this week is the free government tool that explains why that line exists in the first place. FDA's Interactive Nutrition Facts Label walks through a real label section by section, including the "Added Sugars" row specifically, in plain language: what counts as added, why it's broken out separately from total sugars, and why its Daily Value is capped at 50 grams on a 2,000-calorie diet. It won't scan a barcode or total up a day's intake the way Mr Bite's own label format is built to, but it's the clearest free explanation of the one row this issue found Mr Bite has never once added to its own. [FDA's Interactive Nutrition Facts Label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/interactiveNutritionFactsLabel/added-sugars.cfm)
