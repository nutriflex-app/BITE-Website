On October 28th, Food Standards Australia New Zealand reopened public consultation on Proposal P1067, the plan to make the Health Star Rating mandatory on most packaged retail food in Australia and New Zealand. It's the second round, an eight-week window instead of the usual six given how much interest the first round drew, and it runs through December 23rd. FSANZ has published the draft regulatory text, a cost-benefit impact statement, and a report on how the scoring algorithm actually performs across real products. The system itself isn't new, it's been sitting on shelves voluntarily since 2014, but it's never been required, and Food Ministers are the ones who'll eventually vote on whether that changes.

What struck me reading through it is how specific the algorithm is about what it needs. The official HSR calculator, a government-maintained spreadsheet, takes exactly six per-100g (or per-100mL) inputs: energy, saturated fat, total sugars, sodium, fibre, protein, and the percentage of fruit, vegetable, nut, or legume content. Feed it those six numbers for a product and it hands back a rating from half a star to five. It's not a vibe, it's arithmetic, done the same way for every product that gets fed through it.

So I went and checked what Mr Bite's own food data could actually feed into something like that, and the answer is: not much, for the one food type almost every scan produces. `PackagedFood`, the type in `types/index.ts` that every barcode scan and every text search result resolves to, carries exactly six nutrients of its own: calories, protein, carbs, fats, fiber, sugar. No sodium field. No saturated fat field. Not unpopulated, structurally absent, there's nowhere on the type to put either number even if we had it.

And we often do have it, just never looked for. `normalizeOffProduct` in `openFoodFactsService.ts`, the function that turns an Open Food Facts product into a `PackagedFood`, reads `proteins_100g`, `carbohydrates_100g`, `fat_100g`, `fiber_100g`, and `sugars_100g` off the product's `nutriments` object. Open Food Facts' own nutriments object also carries `sodium_100g` and `saturated-fat_100g` as standard fields, the same way it carries the ones we do read, populated for a large share of products. `normalizeOffProduct` never asks for either. The barcode lookup itself pulls the full product document with no `fields` filter, so the numbers are sitting right there in the response on every single scan. Nothing downstream was ever told to keep them.

Here's the part that made me actually sit back: we already built the two missing fields, for a different food entirely. `Recipe` in that same `types/index.ts` carries `spoonacular_sodium` and `spoonacular_saturated_fat`, populated straight from Spoonacular's nutrition data by `spoonacularMapper.ts`, and rendered as real rows, "Sat. Fat" and "Sodium," in the nutrition breakdown on `RecipePreviewScreen.tsx`. It's the identical gating Issue 41 found for the Spoonacular health score badge: the block only runs when `recipe.is_spoonacular`. A recipe pulled from Spoonacular gets a sodium number. A product scanned at a store, the same two nutrients the government calculator treats as non-negotiable, gets nothing, because the type that represents it was never given anywhere to hold them.

Even the search paths make this worse than an oversight. `OFF_SEARCH_V2_FIELDS` and `OFF_SEARCH_CGI_FIELDS`, the explicit field lists Mr Bite sends to Open Food Facts' search endpoints, list out `nutriments` generically, so sodium and saturated fat do come back in a search hit's raw data too. They just land in a candidate object that gets run through the same `normalizeOffProduct`, and vanish the same way.

I'll say the obvious thing before anyone else does: FVNL percentage, the seventh input the calculator actually wants for most categories, doesn't exist as a concept anywhere in this codebase either, and there's no realistic path to it from a barcode alone. I'm not arguing Mr Bite should go build its own Health Star Rating clone by December 23rd. I'm pointing at something smaller and more fixable: four of the six inputs the Australian and New Zealand governments are about to require on a label are either missing from our own data model for the exact food type we scan most, or they exist already, gated to a third party's recipes, sitting unused right next to the thing that needs them.

Worth noting, for anyone tempted to assume Open Food Facts could just hand the finished star rating back instead: it mostly can't. The project's own wiki says the rating lives as a crowdsourced label tag, not something OFF computes from the nutriments itself, and as of their last public count only a few thousand products carried it at all. Even a perfect fields request wouldn't get you a reliable number today. The six inputs would still have to come from the nutriments object, the same six numbers, mostly, that we already know how to read and chose not to, for everything but a Spoonacular recipe.

## Tool of the Week: The Health Star Rating Calculator

![A spreadsheet grid icon with a column of six small nutrient icons, an energy bolt, a fat droplet, a sugar cube, a salt crystal, a fiber stalk, and a protein strand, each connected by a thin line into a single glowing star-rating icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261113_081712_2cf252db-f053-4b4e-aab9-fb1d17fc4e31.png)

Since this issue is about exactly which numbers a mandatory food score actually needs, the pick this week is the free government spreadsheet that does the math in the open. The Health Star Rating Calculator, maintained by the Australian government, is a downloadable Excel tool that takes the same six per-100g inputs FSANZ's proposal is built on and returns the star rating a product would actually get, category rules and lookup tables included, no login or submission required. It won't tell you whether a given barcode's data is complete enough to run through it, that's still on whoever filled in the product page, but it's the clearest free way to see what "mandatory" is actually going to demand of a nutrition panel. [Health Star Rating Calculator](https://www.healthstarrating.gov.au/calculator)
