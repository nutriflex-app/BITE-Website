On November 18th, a Stanford Medicine-led team published a study in *JACC: Advances* asking a question most nutrition advice skips over: does saturated fat raise cholesterol the same way for everyone? They pulled genetic data, dietary questionnaires, and cholesterol measurements for close to 21,000 postmenopausal women enrolled in the Women's Health Initiative, a long-running national health study, and split them by how genetically predisposed each one was to high LDL cholesterol. The answer was no. In women with a strong genetic predisposition, a diet higher in saturated fat drove LDL up sharply. In women without that predisposition, the same diet barely moved it. The researchers went a step further and tied the pattern to outcomes: women in the top quarter for genetic risk who also ate more saturated fat had higher rates of heart attack, stroke, and other cardiovascular events than women in that same top quarter who ate less of it. Shoa Clarke, the study's senior author, was careful about what the finding doesn't say. There's no evidence of a cardiovascular benefit to eating more saturated fat, he said, but there's also no evidence yet that an individual should get genetically tested before deciding what to eat. The study is observational, and the population was limited to postmenopausal women of predominantly European ancestry.

That population detail caught my attention, because it's a population Mr Bite's own code already has a name for. `inferFemaleLifeStageByAge`, in `nutritionAssistantService.ts`, takes a user's age and sorts her into one of three buckets: `cycling_likely` under 45, `perimenopause_possible` up to 54, and `postmenopause_likely` above that, the exact phase of life the Stanford study tracked. It isn't just a backend guess, either. `femaleLifeStage` is one of 48 fields in the `IntakeFieldKey` type in `mrBiteIntakeFields.ts`, the canonical list of everything Mr Bite's onboarding chat is built to collect, sitting alongside `menstrualCyclePhase`, `cycleSymptoms`, and `menopauseSymptoms`, each with its own short memory key and its own plain-language label the coach is instructed to ask about. The chat context assembled for every coaching turn, built in `fetchNutritionAssistantUserContext`, pulls a user's age, sex, and that same life-stage hint and writes it straight into the prompt the model sees: "Female nutrition stage (explicit or age-based hint): ...". That's genuinely more granular personalization than most nutrition apps bother building.

So I went looking for the other half of the Stanford study inside that same onboarding list, the half about genetic or family risk, and it isn't there. I read through all 48 entries in `IntakeFieldKey`. None of them is family history, heart disease, cholesterol, or anything resembling genetic predisposition. I checked all 181 files under `supabase/migrations`, every `CREATE TABLE` and `ALTER TABLE` this app has ever run, the same search Issue 52 ran looking for a caffeine column. Nothing there either. The closest match is `dietaryExclusions`, labeled "allergies, intolerances, and medical restrictions," and that's about what a user won't eat, not about what runs in her family. The word "cholesterol" does appear twice elsewhere in the codebase, but neither use is a data field. In `mr-bite-chat/index.ts`, it's one of roughly forty words in `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE`, the regex that decides whether a chat message needs a sourced, careful answer. In `nutritionAssistantService.ts`, it's one of several words in a separate routing regex that decides whether a request is complex enough to hand off to a bigger model. Both treat "cholesterol" purely as a topic to answer carefully if a user happens to bring it up. Neither treats it as something to ask about in the first place.

I want to be as fair to the limits here as Clarke was to his own study. Nobody is suggesting Mr Bite should run genetic panels, and the researchers themselves aren't recommending that for any individual either. But family history isn't a lab test. It's a question a basic medical intake has asked for decades, and it's a free, five-second version of exactly the signal this study just confirmed matters: whether saturated fat is doing something very different to this specific person than it does to the next one. Mr Bite already asks a postmenopausal user about her menopause symptoms in enough detail to save them to memory. It has just never once asked whether her mother had high cholesterol.

## Three Quick Bites

![Three vignettes in a row: an energy drink can and a small supplement bottle beside a heart icon with a warning mark, a frozen food package beside a crossed-out sesame-seed icon, and a pint of gelato beside a magnifying glass over a glowing bacteria icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261123_081750_794bb791-7d85-4880-91b0-13b877ebe037.png)

1. **A preliminary analysis presented at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions this month found that energy drinks and caffeinated or stimulant supplements showed up far more often than expected in FDA adverse-event reports tied to serious heart problems.** Researchers compared 854 reports naming a caffeinated or stimulant product against more than 27,000 reports that didn't, and found cardiac arrest named about 11 times as often and heart attack about 4.5 times as often in the stimulant group; about 45% of those reports involved people age 18 to 34. The findings are preliminary and haven't yet been published as a peer-reviewed paper. [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261110042543.htm)

2. **USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service issued a public health alert on November 6th for three frozen Betterballen Foods snacks, Bami Rolls, Saté Kroketten, and Bitterballen, after finding they contain sesame that was never declared on the label, with the beef Bitterballen also carrying undeclared fish from a Worcestershire sauce ingredient.** The products were sold only online, nationwide, through Lowlands House, and a recall wasn't requested because none are still available for purchase; no allergic reactions have been reported. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/usda-issues-warning-about-betterballen-beef-and-chicken-products-because-of-undeclared-allergens/)

3. **The FDA classified Lidl's recall of one lot of Gelatelli Coffee & Vanilla Gelato with Coffee Sauce & Cookie Pieces as a Class I risk, its most serious rating, on November 3rd, after the retailer's own quality testing turned up Listeria monocytogenes.** Lidl pulled the pints, sold across Washington, D.C. and nine East Coast states between July and mid-October, as soon as the lab result came back; no illnesses have been reported. [KTVU](https://www.ktvu.com/news/gelato-sold-lidl-stores-multiple-states-recalled-over-possible-listeria-contamination)

## Tool of the Week: CDC's My Family Health Portrait

![A laptop screen showing a simple family-tree icon with a small heart icon at its center and a checkmark icon beside it, a government-building silhouette faint in the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261123_081753_7169d301-a921-4ae0-8483-46b346d773ae.png)

Since this issue is about a question Mr Bite's onboarding never asks, the pick this week is the free government tool built entirely around asking it. CDC's My Family Health Portrait walks you through recording which blood relatives have had which conditions, heart disease and high cholesterol included, then turns it into a shareable summary you can hand to an actual doctor or keep for your own records. It won't calculate a genetic risk score, that's still a clinical question Clarke's own study stops short of settling, but it's the clearest free way to answer the one question a basic medical intake has asked for decades and Mr Bite's 48 onboarding fields still don't. [CDC's My Family Health Portrait](https://www.cdc.gov/family-health-history/resources/index.html)
