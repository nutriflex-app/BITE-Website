Researchers from UCL and Imperial College London published a study this month in *BMC Medicine* asking whether an ultra-processed diet leaves a trace in the blood that a food diary alone can't catch. They drew on more than 6,000 UK adults who'd given blood samples and filled in diet questionnaires, then tracked their health for up to 20 years. Out of 233 metabolites measured in each blood sample, machine learning picked out 34 most strongly linked to an ultra-processed diet and 50 linked to a minimally processed one, with 8 showing up on both lists. The ultra-processed signature leaned toward more triglycerides carried by VLDL particles and less of the healthy fats, omega-3s among them, that the minimally processed signature carried instead. People in the highest third of ultra-processed intake carried a 17% higher cardiovascular disease risk than people in the lowest third over that 20-year follow-up, and the metabolite signature alone accounted for 51% of that heart-disease link and 43% of the link to type 2 diabetes.

The honest caveat, the kind the researchers raised themselves: this is observational, a pattern tracked over two decades, not a mechanism proven in a lab. But the part that actually makes the study interesting is the part worth sitting with. The disease link survived even after the researchers adjusted for the extra sugar, fat, and salt that ultra-processed diets typically carry. Whatever is driving over half of that cardiovascular signal, it isn't fully explained by the numbers already printed on a nutrition label. The researchers' own guess, gut microbes, inflammation, or appetite signals disrupted by the food matrix itself, is still a guess, not a finding.

So I went and looked at what Mr Bite's own code does with the nutrients that ARE printed on a label, the ones a blood test shouldn't even need to catch because they're sitting right there on the package. `NutritionLabel.tsx`, in `src/components/`, is a complete, properly laid out Nutrition Facts panel: calories, total fat, saturated fat, trans fat, cholesterol, sodium, total carbohydrate, fiber, sugars, protein, vitamin A, vitamin C, calcium, iron, fourteen fields in all, built the way the FDA's own label is laid out, down to the "% Daily Value" footnote. It's exported from `src/components/index.ts`, the barrel file every screen in the app imports shared components from. I grepped the whole codebase for `<NutritionLabel` to find out which screen actually renders it. Nothing comes back. The component exists, it compiles, and it has never once been mounted.

What makes that stranger is that a different screen already half-rebuilt a piece of it by hand, without reusing it. `RecipePreviewScreen.tsx` has its own "Extended nutrition" block that pushes custom rows onto a list for fiber, sugar, saturated fat, sodium, vitamin C, iron, and calcium, each one paired with its own hand-picked icon (a droplet for saturated fat, a wave for sodium, a citrus slice for vitamin C), gated behind `recipe.is_spoonacular`. Issue 53 found that same gate sitting on sodium and saturated fat; it turns out it covers five more fields besides. Nobody reused `NutritionLabel.tsx` to build this screen. Somebody wrote seven new rows and picked seven new icons instead, for a screen that still leaves out cholesterol, trans fat, and vitamin A entirely, because Spoonacular's own nutrition data and the `spoonacular_` fields in `types/index.ts` never picked those three up either.

I want to be careful not to overclaim the connection here. The UCL and Imperial signature is built from triglycerides, VLDL particles, and omega-3s measured straight from blood, not from cholesterol or trans fat sitting on a label, and nothing about rendering `NutritionLabel.tsx` would catch the part of this month's finding that's actually new. But the study is a reminder that the stuff printed on a package, the stuff regulators have required for thirty years precisely because it predicts disease risk on its own, is worth getting right before reaching for anything more exotic. Mr Bite built the component for that, in full, a while ago. It's sitting in the repository, compiled, unused, while a different screen quietly reinvents a third of it with less.

## Three Quick Bites

![Three vignettes in a row: a nutrition facts label with a magnifying glass over a sugar-cube icon, a smoked salmon fillet beside a small warning triangle icon, and a wheel of cheddar cheese beside a small warning icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261117_081621_c1a7f0da-6586-4ab2-9e57-0d881ce3073a.png)

1. **A CDC-led analysis of NHANES survey data found that adults who read nutrition labels often consumed about 43 grams less added sugar a day than adults who rarely or never read them.** The analysis covered 9,416 children and adults surveyed between 2017 and March 2020; it's an association pulled from a national survey, not a controlled trial, so it can't rule out that people already eating less sugar are also the ones more inclined to check a label in the first place. [News-Medical](https://news-medical.net/news/20261112/A-43-gram-sugar-gap-separates-adults-who-check-food-labels-from-those-who-rarely-do.aspx)

2. **The FDA assigned its most serious Class I risk rating on November 3rd to two cold-smoked Starfish Seafood products, a Norwegian salmon and a salmon, halibut, and trout trio, after New York state testing turned up possible Listeria monocytogenes.** The recalls, issued by Brooklyn distributor Krasnyi Oktyabr Inc., had quietly started October 6th; the Class I label doesn't mean the recall grew, just that regulators now rate the risk as the kind that can cause serious illness or death. No illnesses have been reported. [ConsumerAffairs](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/fda-assigns-highest-risk-level-to-two-smoked-seafood-recalls-110926.html)

3. **Family Farmstead Dairy recalled all of its Raw Mild and Raw Sharp Cheddar, 8-ounce packages with lot codes dated 10/16/27 or earlier, after the FDA flagged possible Listeria monocytogenes on November 10th.** It's the small creamery's second recall in under a month: the same raw cheddar line was recalled October 15th over two strains of E. coli. The cheese reached retail stores across 12 states, from New York down to Tennessee; no illnesses have been linked to either recall. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/family-farmstead-recalls-more-raw-cheese-this-time-because-of-listeria/)

## Tool of the Week: FDA's Interactive Nutrition Facts Label

![A laptop screen showing a simple nutrition label icon with a magnifying glass over it and a small quiz checkmark icon beside it, a government-building silhouette faint in the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261117_081628_2e991dc7-9a38-4bf8-8d23-9d5d959a178a.png)

Since this issue is about which fields on a nutrition label actually get built, let alone rendered, the pick this week is the free government tool that explains all of them. The FDA's Interactive Nutrition Facts Label walks through every line of the label, cholesterol, trans fat, sodium, the whole list `NutritionLabel.tsx` already has slots for, with plain-language explanations of what each one means and why it's required, plus a short quiz to test what stuck. It won't catch a blood metabolite signature, no label does yet, but it's the clearest free way to understand what the thirty-year-old label was actually built to tell you before worrying about what it still can't. [FDA's Interactive Nutrition Facts Label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/InteractiveNutritionFactsLabel/)
