Nobody in the United States has ever put a federal number on how much caffeine in one serving counts as a lot. The FDA's 2026 guidance agenda lists a planned document titled "Labeling Caffeine Content in Foods and Beverages," but as of this writing that's a line item, not a draft anyone outside the agency has read, and the FDA still has no regulation specific to energy drinks at all. So the actual fight over where "high caffeine" starts has moved entirely to the states, and New Jersey can't even agree with itself. Senate bill S4183, introduced in May, would require a warning wherever a drink hits 80 milligrams of caffeine per serving, posted right at the point of sale, drive-through menu boards included. Senate bill S5008 sets the line at 150 milligrams. The Assembly's version, A3259, came out of the Consumer Affairs Committee on October 5th with an amendment that raised its own threshold to 200 milligrams and narrowed who it even applies to, chains with 20 or more locations. Three bills, one legislature, one session, three different numbers for the same warning label. Pennsylvania, New York, and Delaware all have versions of a similar bill moving through their own committees, according to a law firm's rundown of the fight published this month, and none of those lines up with New Jersey's either.

None of that disagreement is really about whether caffeine is dangerous at some dose, everyone involved agrees it can be. It's that nobody has settled on the one number that would let a label, an app, or a parent say "that's a lot" with any authority behind it. So I went looking for what Mr Bite does with that same question, and found an app that already treats caffeine as worth asking about, in three completely separate places, and has never once turned it into a number anywhere.

The first place is onboarding. `parseAllergiesAnswer` in `biteIntakeAnswers.ts` runs every free-text answer to the allergy question through `INTOLERANCE_RE`, a short list: lactose, gluten, FODMAP, fructose, histamine, caffeine, sorbitol. Say "caffeine" there and Mr Bite correctly routes it to intolerances rather than allergies, the same careful sorting Issue 43 found built for milk. The second is the live chat. `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE` in `mr-bite-chat/index.ts`, the regex that decides a message needs the careful, sourced kind of answer rather than a casual one, lists caffeine right alongside sodium, iron, and blood pressure. The third is the citation system Issue 44 already found only switches on for questions: `mrBiteHealthClaimSources.ts` maps any message containing "caffeine," "coffee," or "energy drinks" straight to a MedlinePlus caffeine page, so when the coach does answer, it answers with a real source attached.

Three places, three deliberate decisions that caffeine deserves careful handling. What's missing is a fourth: a field. `PackagedFood` in `types/index.ts` carries exactly six nutrients, calories, protein, carbs, fats, fiber, sugar, and `Recipe` carries the same six. I checked all 181 files under `supabase/migrations`, every `CREATE TABLE` and `ALTER TABLE` this app has ever run. Not one column anywhere is named anything close to caffeine. Open Food Facts, the database every barcode scan and search result gets normalized from, does define a `caffeine_100g` field, the wiki documents it as a standard per-100g nutriment alongside sugar and sodium, and it's genuinely populated for some products, though plenty of energy drink listings leave it blank and list the number only in the ingredients text instead. It doesn't matter either way here: Issue 48 already established that Mr Bite's barcode lookup requests no `fields` parameter at all, so the full product document, caffeine and all, arrives on every single scan. `normalizeOffProduct` reads `product.nutriments.proteins_100g`, `.carbohydrates_100g`, `.fat_100g`, and a handful of others, and simply never looks for `caffeine_100g`, populated or not. The search paths aren't any better: `OFF_SEARCH_V2_FIELDS` and `OFF_SEARCH_CGI_FIELDS` do ask for the whole `nutriments` object by name, so the number comes back there too when it exists. Nothing downstream was ever told to keep it.

Put the three together and here's what actually happens today: a user tells Mr Bite, during onboarding, that they're caffeine intolerant. Mr Bite stores that, correctly, as an intolerance. That same user then scans a can of something with a real `caffeine_100g` value sitting in the response Open Food Facts just sent back, or orders a coffee drink and asks the coach about it, triggering a careful, sourced reply. At no point does any of that arithmetic happen, because there is no number to add up. Whatever New Jersey, Pennsylvania, New York, and Delaware eventually decide "high caffeine" means, 80 milligrams or 200, Mr Bite's own running total for any user, intolerant or not, is zero, every time, by construction.

1. **The FDA classified two Starfish Seafood cold-smoked products, a Norwegian salmon and a salmon-halibut-trout trio imported by Brooklyn's Krasnyi Oktyabr Inc., as Class I recalls on November 3rd over Listeria monocytogenes.** New York state inspectors found the contamination during routine testing; both products shipped frozen in 8-ounce vacuum-sealed packages with best-by dates running into late 2027, and no illnesses have been reported. 2. **Kayem Foods recalled about 1.58 million pounds of ready-to-eat hot dogs, bacon, sausage, ham, and deli meat in mid-October after a warehouse cooler ran warm enough to let bacteria grow.** The Class I recall spans 68 products across nine brand names, including Kayem, Al Fresco, and Genoa, covering meat made between March 31st and October 7th; no illnesses have been linked to it so far. [Food Safety News](https://foodsafetynews.com/2026/10/more-than-1-5-million-pounds-of-hot-dogs-bacon-sausage-turkey-breasts-and-more-meat-products-of-various-brands-recalled)

## Tool of the Week: USDA FoodData Central

![An open government database webpage icon with a simple coffee cup and a cola can connected by thin lines to a magnifying glass and a chemistry flask icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261111_082146_478d8171-5958-4bfa-878c-3225fb0cb084.png)

Since this issue is about a number nobody's barcode database reliably carries, the pick this week is the free government one that was actually built to measure it. USDA FoodData Central is a searchable, no-login nutrient database that lists caffeine content in milligrams for a wide range of foundation foods, coffee, tea, and chocolate among them, backed by lab analysis rather than a brand's own label. It's weaker on branded, packaged products than on the raw and generic foods in its Foundation and SR Legacy datasets, so a specific can of a specific energy drink is still a label-reading job, but for "how much caffeine is actually in a cup of brewed coffee," it's a far steadier answer than hoping a barcode scan happened to carry the field. [USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/)
