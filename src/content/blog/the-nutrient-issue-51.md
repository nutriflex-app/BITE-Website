On November 6th, the FDA spent three and a half hours in a public meeting titled "Challenges and Solutions in Lot-Level Food Traceability," working through a problem that sounds simple and isn't: how does the specific batch a food was made in stay attached to it as it moves through a warehouse, a distribution center, a store, and eventually someone's kitchen. The rule behind the meeting, the Food Traceability Rule under FSMA Section 204, has been law since 2022. It requires any business handling a food on the FDA's Food Traceability List, things like leafy greens, melons, peppers, soft cheese, shell eggs, and nut butter, to assign that food a traceability lot code and pass it along at every point the food changes hands, so that if a lot sickens people, investigators can trace it back and forward in hours instead of weeks. Congress has told FDA not to enforce it before July 20, 2028, and FDA says it intends to comply with that. Written comments on what the November 6th meeting raised are due December 6th, in a docket that's been open, in one form or another, since 2014.

The gap the meeting spent three and a half hours on is the gap between a product's identity and a specific run of that product. A barcode, the GTIN a store scanner reads, identifies "this brand's 16-ounce bag of this spinach." It's the same number on every bag of it ever printed, from the first production run to the one sitting on a shelf right now. A traceability lot code identifies one specific run: grown on this date, packed on this line, shipped in this batch. Two bags with the identical barcode can carry completely different lot codes, and only one of them might be the one a recall actually names. That's the entire reason the rule exists: a barcode alone can't tell a store, a warehouse, or a shopper which units are the dangerous ones.

Issue 35 already found that Mr Bite has recalled products' barcodes sitting in its own reference data and has never once checked a scan against them. What I went looking for this time is narrower: even if that check got built tomorrow, what would it actually be checking against? I went back to `openFoodFactsService.ts`, the file every barcode scan runs through. `normalizeOffProduct` builds the `PackagedFood` object the rest of the app works with, and its very first field is `food_id: code || barcode`, the GTIN Open Food Facts attached to the scan, nothing else. The `PackagedFood` type in `types/index.ts` has a `category` and `category_label`, both pulled from the product's catalog classification, the same unused fields Issue 49 found computed and thrown away. It has no field for a lot number, a batch code, a pack date, or anything resembling one.

That's not a Mr Bite oversight so much as an Open Food Facts one, and that's the deeper problem. Open Food Facts is a catalog: one entry per barcode, crowdsourced and photographed once, meant to answer "what is this product." It was never built to carry "which specific run of this product is this can," because nobody photographing a label for a public database is equipped to read a lot stamp off a can bottom and nobody downstream is asking for it. A repo-wide search for "lot," "batch," and "traceability" across this codebase turns up onboarding copy about batch-cooking meals and test fixtures about batching database writes, the ordinary meaning of the word in a cooking app. Not one hit refers to a food production lot. The concept isn't missing a field. It isn't in the data Mr Bite's entire packaged-food pipeline is built on top of, so there's nowhere for a field to go.

None of this means Mr Bite needs to become a lot-tracking system; that's squarely FDA, suppliers, and retailers' job, and the rule gives them until 2028 to build it. It means that the recall-matching Issue 35 flagged as unbuilt would, even built today, only ever answer "is this product type one that got recalled," never "is this specific box the one." The FDA spent three and a half hours this week on exactly that distinction. Mr Bite's data has never been able to draw it.

## Three Quick Bites

![Three vignettes in a row: a breakfast biscuit sandwich beside a small warning triangle icon, a container of broccoli sprouts beside a seed packet and a checkmark icon, and a jar of fresh salsa beside a magnifying glass over a glowing bacterium icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261109_081939_6caae4ba-b132-4ed0-85d3-78114a1625f8.png)

1. **Fresh & Ready Foods recalled about 2,414 units of its Fresh To You sausage, egg, and cheese biscuit sandwiches on November 8th after the English muffin component, made by CH Guenther & Son, turned up tied to a Listeria risk.** The sandwiches went to employee break rooms, refrigerated vending machines, and cafeterias in Oregon and Washington; nobody has reported getting sick. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/biscuit-sandwiches-recalled-because-of-listeria-risk/)

2. **The CDC declared a Salmonella Bovismorbificans outbreak linked to broccoli sprouts and microgreens over on November 4th, with a final count of 34 illnesses across six states and three hospitalizations.** Investigators traced it to a single contaminated seed lot imported from New Zealand and grown out by Evergreen Fresh Sprouts of Idaho; three separate companies that sold seed from the same lot for home sprouting have recalled it, so a packet bought months ago could still be sitting in a cabinet. [Medical Daily](https://www.medicaldaily.com/salmonella-outbreak-broccoli-sprouts-recalled-seeds-480912)

3. **An FDA warning letter posted November 3rd, dated July 29th, found Listeria monocytogenes on multiple surfaces inside La Cabana Food Distributing's Oceanside, California plant, which makes ready-to-eat salsas and sauces with no cooking step.** The letter says the company wasn't testing its own environmental surfaces for the bacteria at all; no illnesses have been linked to the plant, and the strain found didn't match any known outbreak. [Medical Daily](https://www.medicaldaily.com/fda-listeria-oceanside-salsa-plant-480889)

## Tool of the Week: FDA's Food Traceability List

![An open government webpage icon with a simple list of food icons, a leaf, a melon, an egg, each tagged with a small code label, connecting by a line to a shopping cart icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261109_081941_bfbbd54c-2c9a-4fd3-bc8a-cb29fbfb99ee.png)

Since this issue is about which foods even carry a traceability lot code in the first place, the pick this week is the free FDA page that actually lists them: the Food Traceability List, the definitive roster of which foods, from leafy greens to shell eggs to certain seafood, are legally required to carry one once enforcement starts. It won't tell you whether the specific bag in your fridge is from a recalled lot, that's still on the label or the store, but it's the honest starting point for "is this even the kind of food this rule covers," which beats guessing from a recall headline about a completely different product. [FDA Food Traceability List](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-safety-modernization-act-fsma/food-traceability-list)
