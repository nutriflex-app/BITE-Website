A 12-week trial out of Texas A&M, published in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* and funded through the Texas A&M Foundation's WoodNext Foundation, asked a simple question: does creatine still do anything if you don't lift. Sixty-four healthy but sedentary adults between 45 and 65 chose whether to join a structured exercise-and-diet program, then were randomized within that choice to either creatine monohydrate or a maltodextrin placebo. The dose was 10 grams a day, split into two 5-gram servings, not the 3 to 5 grams most creatine users have been told for years is the maintenance amount. Among the people who did no structured exercise at all, the creatine group still gained roughly 1.1 kilograms, about 2.4 pounds, of lean tissue over 12 weeks, plus measurable gains in leg and bench press strength, against a placebo group that didn't move. The group doing creatine alongside exercise and diet did better still, nearly 3 pounds of lean tissue and a real drop in body fat percentage.

The honest caveats matter here as much as the headline. The no-exercise creatine arm that drove the "even without exercise" finding was small, 13 people. Lean tissue measured by DXA scanning isn't the same thing as muscle, and creatine is well known to pull water into muscle cells, which can inflate a lean-mass number that has nothing to do with new tissue. Exercise participation was self-selected rather than randomized, so the groups weren't identical going in. None of that erases the finding, it just means the number that matters here, the dose, is doing real work: 10 grams a day produced results that the 3-to-5-gram maintenance dose most people actually take was never tested against in this trial, and the two are not interchangeable just because both get called "taking creatine."

So I went and checked what happens when a Mr Bite user actually tells the coach they're taking it. The app treats creatine as a subject worth real care, in more than one place. `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE` in `mr-bite-chat/index.ts`, the regex that routes a message into the slower, sourced kind of answer instead of a casual one, lists creatine right alongside protein, iron, and blood sugar. `mrBiteHealthClaimSources.ts` goes further: any message matching `vitamin`, `mineral`, `supplement`, or `creatine` gets handed MedlinePlus's vitamins page and the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements as sources before the coach is allowed to say anything about it. This isn't a token gesture, it's the same verification step Issues 43 and 44 found wired into the app's other health claims, and creatine is explicitly inside it.

Here's where it stops. `bite_memory_add`, the tool the coach calls to write a durable fact about a user, takes exactly two fields: a section, and a one-sentence free-text `note`, capped at 240 characters, third person. Nothing in that schema blocks a number from appearing in the note. What blocks it is a sentence sitting in `biteGlobalRules.ts`, duplicated on purpose in both the app and the edge function so every prompt path gets the same instruction: memory should save "supplement names only (never dosing)." That's not a missing database column, the kind of gap most issues here turn up. It's a rule the team wrote on purpose, and I think the instinct behind it is defensible: an AI nutrition coach quoting back a specific gram number starts to look like it's issuing a dosing recommendation, which is exactly the kind of territory Mr Bite's global rules elsewhere go out of their way to avoid. The one other place a supplement shows up in onboarding, `supplementTiming` in `mrBiteIntakeFields.ts`, only ever captures "supplements that affect meal timing," a note about when, never how much.

Put it together and here's the actual gap: a user can tell Mr Bite "I started taking creatine," the coach will correctly save that, route a follow-up question about it through source verification, and cite MedlinePlus and the NIH. If that same user says "I'm doing 10 grams a day, split in two," the rule tells the coach to keep the first half of that sentence and drop the second, every time. A study that exists specifically because the dose changed the outcome arrives at an app that was told, as a matter of policy, never to remember the dose. I'm not arguing Mr Bite should start recommending gram amounts, that line is worth holding. But there's a real difference between refusing to tell someone what dose to take and refusing to remember the one they already told you, and right now the rule draws no distinction between the two.

1. **USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service issued a public health alert on November 6th for three frozen Betterballen snack products, Bami Rolls, Saté Kroketten, and Bitterballen, after finding undeclared sesame on all three and undeclared anchovy, carried in from Worcestershire sauce, on the beef item.** The products, produced October 15th, 16th, and 27th and sold nationwide through Lowlands House's online store, weren't recalled because they're no longer being sold; no reactions have been reported. [FSIS](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USFSIS/bulletins/431fbac)

2. **Fresh & Ready Foods recalled 2,414 Fresh To You Sausage, Egg & Cheese Biscuit Sandwiches, sold in break rooms and vending machines across Washington and Oregon, after its supplier flagged a possible Listeria contamination in the biscuit itself.** The FDA posted the notice November 8th; the biscuit was used only in this one sandwich, and no illnesses have been reported. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/biscuit-sandwiches-recalled-because-of-listeria-risk/)

3. **Canada's food inspection agency recalled T&T brand Organic Roasted Black Soybeans, sold in 300-gram jars, after testing found undeclared milk on a label that never listed it.** The recall, posted the weekend of November 7th to 9th, covers the product nationally; no allergic reactions have been reported to the recalling company, Uncle T Food, so far. ## Tool of the Week: NIH's Dietary Supplement Label Database

![An open laptop showing a simple supplement label icon with a magnifying glass over its ingredient list and small measurement tick marks beside it, a government-building silhouette faint in the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261115_081601_af8ac886-9df6-453f-b5c2-5acc71583b85.png)

Since this issue is about a dose that got dropped on the way into an app's memory, the pick this week is the free government database built to hold exactly that number. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements' Dietary Supplement Label Database is a searchable, no-login archive of more than 136,000 U.S. supplement labels, current and discontinued, each one showing the label image, every ingredient, and the exact amount printed for it. It won't tell you whether 10 grams of creatine is right for you, that's still a conversation worth having with someone qualified, but it will tell you exactly what's actually in the tub on your counter, which is the number most apps, Mr BITE's own memory rule included, never ask to keep. [Dietary Supplement Label Database](https://dsld.od.nih.gov/)
