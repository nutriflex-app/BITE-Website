On November 16th, the FDA proposed a rule that would let N-acetyl-L-cysteine, NAC for short, be sold as a dietary supplement for the first time since the agency decided decades ago that it couldn't be. NAC was approved as a prescription drug back in 1963, and under the law that alone was enough to exclude it from the supplement definition, no matter how many stores already had it on the shelf. FDA spent years sending warning letters over it anyway, then backed off in 2022 into a standing policy of enforcement discretion while it figured out what to actually do. This proposed rule would use, for the first time, the rulemaking authority written into that exclusion clause itself, and the reason FDA gave is almost anticlimactic: after a comprehensive safety review, the agency says it did not identify a safety concern with NAC in or as a dietary supplement. If finalized, FDA intends to withdraw the enforcement discretion guidance that's been standing in for an actual answer ever since. Comments are open until January 19th, 2027.

That's a bigger deal than the dry phrase "rulemaking authority" makes it sound. CRN, the supplement industry's own trade group, had petitioned FDA on exactly this question and FDA denied that specific petition in September 2025; this proposed rule reflects some of the same concerns without being a reversal on the record. And "no safety concerns" is doing real work in that sentence. It isn't FDA saying NAC does anything in particular, for antioxidant support, recovery, or whatever a label ends up claiming once this clears. It's FDA saying the ingredient itself, taken as an ingredient, didn't turn up a reason to keep the door shut. Those are different questions, and conflating them is exactly the kind of claim an app that talks to people about supplements needs to get right.

So I went and checked what happens inside Mr Bite the day a user actually types "NAC" into the chat. The coach's global rules, in `biteGlobalRules.ts`, are explicit that health, nutrition, and supplement claims need backup: before saying anything factual on those topics, the model is told to call `verify_health_claim_sources` and use only what comes back. There's also a safety net behind that instruction, in case the model forgets to call it. `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE`, a long regex in `mr-bite-chat/index.ts` that decides whether a message counts as a health question at all, lists creatine, vitamin, mineral, and supplement by name, right alongside protein and blood sugar. If a user's message matches that list and asks a question, the turn gets flagged, and a second check, `isHealthQATurnNeedingSources`, runs the same regex against the model's own reply before letting it ship without citations.

I grepped the entire codebase, case-insensitive, for "NAC" and for "acetylcysteine." Nothing comes back. Not in `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE`, and not in the deterministic fallback list in `mrBiteHealthClaimSources.ts` that exists specifically to catch misses like this one, the same list Issue 54 found already naming creatine, iron, calcium, magnesium, zinc, b12, and folate by name so a health answer never ships without at least a MedlinePlus link attached. A message like "is NAC safe" still gets caught, because "safe" is its own trigger word elsewhere in the regex. But "what does NAC actually do" or "should I be taking NAC" matches no topic word at all. Those turns fall through every gate this app has built specifically to guarantee a citation, not because anyone decided NAC didn't deserve one, but because the word simply isn't on the list anyone wrote.

I don't think this is a case of the rule being wrong on purpose, the way Issue 54 found with creatine dosing. It's closer to the opposite problem: the rule is right, the regex is just old. Creatine, vitamin, mineral, and supplement got written in when they were the obvious words people would type. NAC has spent six decades as a gray-market ingredient FDA mostly tolerated in practice while never actually clearing it, which is exactly the kind of thing that doesn't make it onto anyone's list of "words a nutrition coach should recognize" until the week the agency changes its mind. That week just happened. If this rule finalizes, NAC moves from a quiet workaround sold by habit to an ingredient with an actual FDA blessing, and more people are going to ask an AI coach about it by name instead of saying "supplement" and letting the generic case catch it. Right now, Mr Bite's own failsafe wouldn't know to answer that one with a source.

## Three Quick Bites

1. **The FDA revoked color-additive authorizations on November 9th for three solvents, methylene chloride, ethylene dichloride, and trichloroethylene, used to manufacture annatto extract, paprika oleoresin, and turmeric oleoresin, and separately pulled methylene chloride's approval as a diluent in inks that mark fruits and vegetables, citing lab evidence that it causes cancer in animals.** Both revocations take effect January 11th, 2027; food colored with annatto, paprika, or turmeric stays legal, since only the manufacturing solvents were removed. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/fda-moves-to-revoke-permission-for-some-chemicals-used-in-food-dyes/)

2. **Alamance Foods recalled one batch of Kirkland Signature Real Heavy Whipped Cream sold at Costco in 12 states after members reported a chemical smell and taste, warning the cans may contain a cleaning chemical.** The affected 15-ounce three-packs were sold October 11th through 31st with a best-by date of April 2nd, 2027, and can be returned to Costco for a full refund; no serious illnesses have been reported. [ConsumerAffairs](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/whipped-cream-sold-at-costco-recalled-over-possible-chemical-contamination-111626.html)

3. **USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service issued a public health alert on November 14th warning that ready-to-eat meat products made with biscuits recalled by C.H. Guenther & Son, Mmm! Meals Made in Minutes Biscuit & Sausage Gravy among them, may carry Listeria monocytogenes.** It's a downstream alert tied to a recall that already pulled Fresh To You's sausage biscuit sandwiches off shelves in Oregon and Washington, and FSIS says more products could be added as it traces where the biscuits went; no illnesses have been reported. [USDA FSIS](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USFSIS/bulletins/432d23f)

## Tool of the Week: FDA's Health Fraud Product Database

![A laptop screen showing a simple warning-label icon with a magnifying glass over it and a small shield icon beside it, a government-building silhouette faint in the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261119_081444_c7d23337-1417-4694-937a-eed5d25fa90b.png)

Since this issue is about an ingredient on its way to earning a real FDA blessing, the pick this week is the free government database built to catch the ones that never will. FDA's Health Fraud Product Database logs every product the agency has caught lying about what's actually in the bottle, mostly ones marketed for weight loss, muscle building, or sexual enhancement, spiked with prescription drug ingredients the label never mentions. It won't tell you whether NAC is right for you, that rule is still being written, but it's the clearest free way to check whether the specific bottle on your shelf is one FDA has already caught lying. [FDA Health Fraud Product Database](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/health-fraud-scams/health-fraud-product-database)
