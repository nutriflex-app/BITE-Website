A quiet legal fight over one FDA number has been picking up class actions all year, and a law firm alert published this month says the exposure is still spreading. The rule, 21 C.F.R. 101.9(c)(7), says that when a food's label declares a percent daily value for protein, or makes a claim like "high protein," the company can't just state the raw gram count from a lab test. It has to run that number through a correction, the Protein Digestibility-Corrected Amino Acid Score, or PDCAAS, which multiplies the declared protein by a 0 to 1.0 score for how well the body actually absorbs and uses it. A whey isolate scores close to 1.0. A collagen or pea-blend product can score much lower. The percent daily value on the label is supposed to be that corrected number divided by the 50-gram reference value, not the raw gram count a nitrogen test spits out.

Plaintiffs' firms have been suing over exactly the gap between those two numbers all year. Ryse was sued in August over its Clear Protein drinks. Perfect Bar's case, alleging its panel skips the corrected figure entirely, was still being amended as of this February. Three Wishes Cereal and Kettle and Fire have both faced the same claim. One side argues federal law preempts these suits because FDA lets companies choose either the nitrogen method or PDCAAS; the other side keeps finding judges willing to let the claims proceed anyway. Nobody outside the litigation has settled which argument wins. What both sides agree on is the underlying math: raw grams and corrected grams are different numbers, the law cares which one backs a claim, and a lot of labels only ever show the first one.

That's a narrower, more mechanical version of the food-quality fights this newsletter keeps finding sitting half-built in Mr Bite's own code, so I went looking for whether "protein" means anything more precise than raw grams anywhere in this app. A repo-wide search for "PDCAAS," "digestib," and "corrected protein" returns nothing. Not a comment, not a migration, not a dead function. The concept doesn't exist here.

It's not that Mr Bite is missing some obscure field. It's that "protein" already does a lot of visible, user-facing work in this app, and all of it rests on one uncorrected number. `openFoodFactsService.ts` pulls a barcode scan's protein straight from Open Food Facts' `proteins_100g` and `proteins_serving` fields, the same nitrogen-method total the lawsuits are about, with `macroPer100PreferServing` doing nothing more than a unit conversion. `PackagedFood` and the recipe `Recipe` type each carry exactly one protein number, no quality adjustment, no %DV field at all anywhere in the schema.

Then the app builds marketing claims directly on top of that one number. `BrowseRecipesScreen.tsx` defines a "Protein Snack Fix" shelf, subtitle "High-protein bites under 300 kcal," that's really just `minProtein: 20`, a raw-gram cutoff. "Post-Gym Plates," subtitled "Protein-forward carb plates," is `minProtein: 30`. "Sweet Protein Treats" is the same cutoff again on dessert-tagged recipes. None of these thresholds know whether the 20 grams in question came from whey or from a dessert bar's pea-protein blend; the filter can't tell the difference, because the field it reads never carried that information in the first place. Onboarding does it too: type "high protein" into the free-text food-preferences screen and `foodReality.ts` writes `proteinPreferences: ['high-protein']` straight into the user's profile, no correction involved. `NutritionGapAnalyzer.tsx` coaches "Add a high-protein meal or snack" off the same raw shortfall, and `getHighProteinSuggestions` in `mealSuggestions.ts` ranks recipes by sorting that same `recipe.protein` field in descending order, highest raw number first.

Mr Bite isn't a packaged-goods company with a Nutrition Facts panel, so the FDA rule driving this year's lawsuits doesn't apply to it directly, it has no label for a plaintiff's attorney to photograph. But the word doing the work, "high-protein," shows up as a shelf title, an onboarding tag, a coaching nudge, and a sort key, in an app built specifically to help someone eat enough of a nutrient whose quality this entire legal fight says isn't captured by the gram count alone. None of this means Mr Bite needs its own PDCAAS calculator before the next recipe shelf ships. It means the one number backing five different "high-protein" claims across this app is precisely the number a year of class actions says isn't enough, on its own, to back one.

## BITE of the Week

![A weekly calendar card made of simple meal icons, a plate, a bowl, a wrap, arranged across seven small day columns, a shopping cart icon branching off to one side, and a chef's hat icon above a single checkmark, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261107_081454_fc1ffef5-1290-4265-9bc4-7e8fb67e03e7.png)

A big one this time. For the past 60 days, Mr Bite's meal planner worked turn by turn: 114 planning conversations, 83 of them spent saving one meal per tool call, 13 calls failing on missing details like a week start date that wasn't a Monday. Only 12 of 14 draft plans ever got saved, and of the 26 planned days that had three or more meals in them, the median came in at 86 percent of the calorie target, with 11 landing under 80 percent. Across those two months, exactly two plans got saved. Even the founder's own test run logged 1,292 calories against a 2,842 target in a single session, because the goal field had gone missing and the model guessed "maintain."

Meal planning v3, merged to `main` on November 6th, flips who does what. The model still picks the dishes, variety, taste, which days to batch-cook, but code now owns the arithmetic: portions get sized in quarter steps so every day lands within 5 percent of target, snacks and sides get filled in from a library of simple foods, and the whole week saves in one write instead of thirty. What used to take dozens of tool calls now takes two. In the app, that shows up as a week calendar card under Mr Bite's chat reply, a new Days and Cooking and Shopping plan screen, a "Today's plan" card on Home, and a Plans library that opens straight into the week.

It's merged, not shipped. The build touches a Supabase migration, the `mr-bite-chat` server function, and pure JS/TS, no native module changes, so it's a founder-run deploy and migration push away from being live, eligible for an over-the-air push rather than a full store build. The last version bump was 1.18.1 on November 2nd, four days before this landed, so if your weekly plan still looks like the old one-meal-at-a-time version, that's why.

## Three Quick Bites

![Three vignettes in a row: a bag of kale chips beside a small warning triangle icon, a toddler soft-baked snack bar beside a small choking-hazard icon, and a jar of ground cinnamon beside a small hazard symbol, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261107_081456_0161ba2f-3d51-46ee-b9d4-83d8165b6bb4.png)

1. **Snack Right Super Foods recalled its Brad's Plant Based Crunchy Kale and Brad's Veggie Chips Kale products in early November after FDA testing confirmed thallium contamination the company had been told about since a California warning back in May.** The toxic metal can cause hair loss, nerve pain in the hands and feet, and heart problems at high exposure, and the recalled bags carry best-by dates running into early 2027. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/kale-chips-recalled-because-of-thallium-contamination/)

2. **The FDA warned on October 30th that Once Upon a Farm's Tractor Wheels toddler soft-baked bars can form a dry, sticky mass in a child's throat that standard first aid, including back blows and the Heimlich maneuver, may fail to clear.** The recall covers every flavor, lot, and expiration date after the company logged reports of toddlers choking, some requiring hospital observation; refunds are available by returning unopened packages. [Scripps News](https://www.abc15.com/life/recalls/toddler-snack-sold-nationwide-recalled-over-choking-risk)

3. **SLR Food Distribution recalled its Wise Wife brand ground cinnamon in late October after testing by the Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station found elevated lead levels.** The 1.76-ounce jars were sold at retailers across several states, timed right before the holiday baking season when cinnamon use spikes; no illnesses have been reported. [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/slr-food-distribution-inc-recalls-wise-wife-brand-ground-cinnamon-because-possible-health-risk)

## Tool of the Week: eCFR's Text of 21 C.F.R. 101.9

![An open government code-of-regulations webpage icon connecting by a simple line to a nutrition label icon with a percent sign on it, a small magnifying glass hovering nearby, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261107_081458_5691a2d8-83bc-4b52-8da0-d3dbee752a3d.png)

Since this issue is about a rule most coverage only paraphrases, the pick this week is the free government page that carries the actual text: the Electronic Code of Federal Regulations' copy of 21 C.F.R. 101.9, searchable and current, with subsection (c)(7) spelling out exactly how a corrected protein figure and its percent daily value are supposed to be calculated. It won't tell you whether any specific label on your shelf got the math right, that takes a lab, but it's the honest, verifiable starting point for "what does the rule actually require," which beats taking a law firm's paraphrase or a plaintiff's complaint at its word. [eCFR, Title 21, Section 101.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)
