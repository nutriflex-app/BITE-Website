A National Cancer Institute epidemiologist named Erikka Loftfield published a study on October 15th built to settle something the "ultra-processed food is bad" headlines keep skipping past: does it matter what kind of ultra-processed food you eat, not just how much. Her team pulled from the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study, 461,682 adults aged 50 to 71, followed for a median of 18.8 years, and checked total ultra-processed intake against who went on to develop colorectal cancer.

The headline result is almost anticlimactic: overall ultra-processed food intake was not associated with a higher risk of colorectal cancer. If that were the whole study, it would be a mild rebuttal of the NOVA-is-destiny narrative and nothing more. But the authors didn't stop at the top-line number, they broke it out by food type, and that's where it gets specific. Ultra-processed whole grains and dairy were associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence. Ultra-processed meat was associated with higher incidence. The same NOVA bucket, "ultra-processed," contains foods pulling in opposite directions on the same outcome. The authors even ran the substitution math: swapping 10 percent of a person's ultra-processed food weight for minimally processed food was associated with about a 2 percent lower incidence, but only because of which foods that 10 percent actually displaces.

That's a falsifiable, specific claim: a single processing category is the wrong level of resolution. What matters is the food type underneath it. So I went looking for every place in Mr Bite's own codebase where we already split food by type, since if that claim is right, any app sorting food into categories should be able to tell a chicken breast from a strip of bacon even before it gets near a processing label.

We can. There's a file called `src/utils/ingredientCategorizer.ts`, with a comment at the top laying out exactly five buckets: Produce, Protein, Dairy, Spices, Pantry. Its main function, `categorizeIngredient()`, runs a cascade of regex checks against an ingredient string and returns one of those five labels. The Protein rule is one line: `/chicken|beef|pork|lamb|fish|salmon|tuna|shrimp|turkey|bacon|sausage|meat|tofu/i`. Read that line again. Bacon and sausage, the two words doing the heavy lifting in this week's "ultra-processed meat raises risk" finding, sit in the exact same regex as chicken, tofu, and salmon, the proteins this week's study has no bad news about at all. The function doesn't distinguish them because it was never asked to. It was asked to sort a shopping list.

And that's genuinely all it's for. `categorizeIngredient()` is called from exactly one place, `coerceShoppingAisle()` in `src/services/shoppingListService.ts`, which runs on every ingredient added to a shopping list from a recipe, a meal plan, or a manual quick-add. The label it produces travels to exactly one destination: `getIngredientCategoryColors()` in `ShoppingListScreen.tsx`, which maps "Protein" to a red-tinted badge so the list visually clusters by aisle. That's the whole trip. I grepped the rest of the codebase, every coaching prompt, every plan builder, every file that touches `CoachDossier`, for "processed meat," "red meat," "bacon," "sausage." Outside that one regex line and two unrelated comments about parsing text like "200g bacon" for quantity math, there's nothing. The categorization that could, in principle, flag a food type this week's study has real evidence against exists, runs on every logged ingredient, and never travels anywhere near the coach.

I don't think the fix is to build a competing health score here either, that's the same trap last issue's Spoonacular badge fell into, a single number standing in for something that needs more resolution, not less. The fix is smaller and more honest: we already pay the cost of classifying every ingredient by type. Right now that classification buys us aisle colors on a shopping list and nothing else. It wouldn't take a new pipeline to let `coerceShoppingAisle()`'s output answer one more question it's already halfway to answering: not "is this processed," but "is this the specific food type this week's actual evidence is about." The data to tell bacon from chicken breast is sitting in the same line of code that currently treats them as interchangeable.

## Tool of the Week: ingredient-parser-nlp

![A handwritten recipe card with a single highlighted ingredient line passing through a small glowing scanner wand, splitting into three separated labeled tag chips above it, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261023_081741_c4a5a93f-e015-4c96-b472-a9ece7163067.png)

Since this issue is about a hand-rolled regex doing a classification job it was never built to do precisely, the pick this week is for the builders reading: `ingredient-parser-nlp`, a free, open-source Python package that uses a trained model, not keyword matching, to pull structured quantity, unit, name, and comment fields out of a raw recipe ingredient sentence. Its documented benchmarks report around 92 percent sentence-level and 97 percent word-level accuracy on held-out test data, the kind of number a regex cascade can't produce because nobody measures a regex's accuracy, they just keep adding cases to it until the bug reports slow down. It's not a nutrition tool and it won't tell you bacon from chicken either, but it's exactly the shape of fix for the other half of this problem: structured food data needs a parser that was actually evaluated, not one that's still growing. [ingredient-parser-nlp on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/ingredient-parser-nlp/)
