On October 9th, the FDA finalized a rule that lets manufacturers use salt substitutes in foods covered by standards of identity that currently require or permit only salt, sodium chloride, and nothing else. It was published in the Federal Register on October 13th and takes effect December 14th. The rule directly amends 80 standards and, through cross-references, reaches 139 total: bread, self-rising flour, a long list of cheeses, macaroni and noodle products, canned vegetables, mayonnaise, salad dressing. It doesn't name a specific substitute. It defines one functionally, as any safe and suitable ingredient that can replace some or all of the added sodium chloride, which in practice means potassium chloride is the obvious candidate, since it's the cheapest and most common one already on the market.

The FDA's own rulemaking says why this isn't a free lunch. Potassium chloride tastes bitter and metallic at high doses, which limits how much of it manufacturers will actually use, and more importantly, it's a real risk for a specific population: people with chronic kidney disease or heart failure who have to watch their potassium intake because their bodies can't clear it normally. The agency considered requiring a warning label on foods made with potassium-based substitutes and decided against it. Existing Nutrition Facts rules already require potassium to be declared when it's present in a meaningful amount, and the FDA apparently decided that's enough disclosure. CSPI, which otherwise welcomed the rule as a real tool for sodium reduction, said as much in its own statement: the strategy needs to account for the people who can't just treat more potassium as neutral or good.

So the number that used to not matter much, because almost nothing had much potassium chloride in it, is about to start mattering in a new set of foods, with no flag on the front of the package to tell you which ones. That's exactly the kind of gap a tracking app is supposed to close for someone who can't just eyeball it. I went and checked whether Mr Bite could.

It can't, because potassium has never existed anywhere in this codebase. I mean that literally: a repo-wide, case-insensitive search for "potassium," across every file, every migration, every test, returns nothing. Not one hit. Compare that to sodium, which at least has a fragmented, half-built presence: it's a field in the Spoonacular recipe type, `spoonacular_sodium`, rendered as an extra row on some recipe preview screens; it's a keyword in two separate regexes, `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE` in both `mr-bite-chat/index.ts` and `mrBiteChatLoopPolicy.ts`, that decide whether the coach's reply needs a citation; and it has its own dedicated source match in `mrBiteHealthClaimSources.ts`, pointing a sodium question at MedlinePlus. Sodium is scattered and incomplete, but it's there. Potassium isn't scattered. It just isn't.

The barcode path makes the gap concrete. `openFoodFactsService.ts` requests the entire `nutriments` object from Open Food Facts in one field, so when a scanned product reports `potassium_100g`, that number is already sitting in the raw API response the app receives. Nothing downstream ever reads it. The function that pulls macros out of that blob, `macroPer100PreferServing`, is called exactly five times, once each for protein, carbs, fat, fiber, and sugar, and never for potassium or sodium. The data arrives and gets thrown away before it's parsed, every single scan.

And the coach that's supposed to answer a question like "can I eat this" has no language for it either. `HEALTH_QA_TOPIC_RE` lists sodium and salt by name, along with a long tail of other nutrients, but not potassium, not even generically under "minerals." A user managing kidney disease who asks Mr Bite about a newly-reformulated loaf of bread is asking a system that would, at best, treat the question as vaguely health-related and reach for a citation about something else.

This connects to a gap Issue 45 already found: the `medical_condition_or_medication` health-safety reason that's supposed to flag exactly this kind of user changes the coaching mode to `clinician_required` and nothing more. It carries no detail about which condition, which means even a user who'd correctly triggered that gate would be talking to a coach with no variable to watch for the thing their condition actually requires watching. The FDA just created a food landscape this app has no instrument for reading, for a population it has a label for and no data for.

The fix isn't exotic. Open Food Facts is already handing us the number for free. USDA FoodData Central, in the Tool of the Week below, would fill in what Open Food Facts doesn't have. But "the data is available" and "we track it" are different sentences, and right now only the first one is true.

## Tool of the Week: USDA FoodData Central

![An open database cylinder icon branching into small labeled nutrient icons, connecting out to a simple phone app icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261031_081557_cf398ad3-81c2-4205-a359-ac11ab56acaa.png)

Since this issue is about a nutrient that's missing everywhere, the pick this week is the free, authoritative source that already has it: USDA FoodData Central, a public API covering branded, standard-reference, and survey foods with full nutrient panels, potassium and sodium both included down to the milligram, sourced from the USDA's own lab analysis and label data rather than crowd-submitted entries. It's not a replacement for Open Food Facts, which has far better barcode coverage for packaged goods off the shelf, but it's exactly the kind of fallback a food-tracking app should reach for when the primary source is thin or missing a field outright, which, as of this week, is precisely our situation with potassium. [FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/)
