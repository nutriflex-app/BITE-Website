On November 17th, FoodNavigator ran a piece making an argument that's become common in food-industry trade coverage this year: as more people take GLP-1 medications and eat less by design, the food they do eat has to work harder. The piece's framing is blunt about the mechanism. Manufacturers, it says, can help minimize the nutrient gaps that come with reduced eating by offering products with higher densities of protein, fiber, and micronutrients, not more food, just more nutrition packed into the smaller amount a GLP-1 user actually eats. The hard part, the piece notes, is raising nutrient density enough to matter while still shipping something people want to eat. It also flags, carefully, that some research has hinted GLP-1 use may shift gut microbiome composition, a finding the piece is honest enough to call a hint rather than a settled fact.

It's a food-manufacturing story, about what goes into a product before it reaches a shelf. But "nutrient gap" is also, almost word for word, the name of a feature Mr Bite's own codebase already has. So I went and checked what that feature actually catches.

`NutritionGapAnalyzer.tsx`, in `src/components/`, takes a day's logged meals and is supposed to tell a user where their eating fell short. It calls a function, `analyzeNutritionGaps`, from `src/utils/mealSuggestions.ts`, and that function defines its own universe of what a "gap" can even be. The type is explicit: `type: 'protein' | 'calories' | 'variety';`. That's the entire list. A calorie gap fires if you're more than 100 calories under target. A protein gap fires if you're more than 10 grams under a protein target, and only if a protein target was passed in at all. The third type, "variety," isn't a nutrient gap in any real sense; it just flags whether you ate the same two or three recipes too many times that day. Fiber isn't a case this type can hold. Neither is any vitamin or mineral. The interface was never built wide enough to notice them, so there was never a bug to fix; the gap simply isn't a shape the code knows how to represent.

That alone would make this a smaller, quieter version of what Issue 55 found in `NutritionLabel.tsx`, a nutrient a feature doesn't check. But this one goes a step further. I grepped the whole codebase for `<NutritionGapAnalyzer`, the only way a React component like this actually reaches a user, since importing a file isn't the same as rendering it. Nothing comes back. Not one screen in the app mounts this component. `analyzeNutritionGaps` has exactly one caller in the entire codebase, and it's this same unused component calling it. The function computes real numbers, pulls real daily totals, sorts gaps by severity, and the result goes nowhere. It's not that Mr Bite tells users about calorie and protein gaps but misses fiber. It's that Mr Bite built the machinery to tell users about calorie and protein gaps, wired it up correctly, and then never put it on a screen.

There's a small, almost funny detail buried in the component's own styling. Down in its footer text, it renders the line "Check Smart Suggestions below," as if it were designed to sit directly above some other feature that would turn a protein gap into an actual recipe recommendation. I don't know if that companion screen ever existed or if it's still waiting to be built, but the component was clearly written assuming a context it has never actually been placed in.

I want to be fair about what this isn't. Mr Bite doesn't track GLP-1 use at all, so none of this is really about serving that specific population; the FoodNavigator piece is about product formulation, not about what a coaching app says to a user, and I'm not claiming otherwise. But the industry has landed on "nutrient gap" as the right frame for a real problem: people eating less still need the same protein, fiber, and micronutrients, just packed tighter. Mr Bite has a component with that exact name, built around exactly that idea, and it can't see fiber because the code was never asked to look for it, and it can't reach a user because nothing in the app ever opened the door for it to try.

## Three Quick Bites

![Three vignettes in a row: a tortelloni pasta package beside a crossed-out bacteria icon, a breakfast sandwich beside a small warning triangle icon, and a bowl of husk-like fiber beside a small intestine icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261121_081436_6dafc3b8-61d5-49a9-b83e-6a7cdaddcd33.png)

1. **ALDI recalled its Priano Protein Carbonara Tortelloni and Priano Protein Herb & Cheese Tortelloni, 9-ounce lot T9101926 with a best-by date of December 18th, 2026, after determining the pasta could support the growth of Clostridium botulinum if not stored and handled properly.** The company announced the recall on November 16th, the FDA posted it the next day, and the affected packages were sold at ALDI stores in Alabama, Georgia, North Carolina, and South Carolina; no illnesses have been reported. [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/11/aldi-recalls-tortelloni-because-of-botulism-risk/)

2. **Fresh & Ready Foods expanded its Listeria recall on November 18th to cover a second Fresh To You Sausage, Egg & Cheese Biscuit Sandwich and a Mmm! Meals Made in Minutes biscuit-and-sausage-gravy meal, both made with biscuits already recalled by supplier C.H. Guenther & Son.** The newly added products were sold through vending machines, break rooms, and micro-markets across seven Southern states; Listeria poses its most serious risk to pregnant people, who can face miscarriage or stillbirth, and no illnesses have been reported so far. [WJLA](https://wjla.com/news/nation-world/recall-alert-fda-expands-recall-of-breakfast-sandwiches-over-bug-linked-to-stillbirths-us-food-and-drug-administration-biscuits-contamination-sausage-egg-cheese-gravy-fresh-ready-foods-lsiteria-pregnant-pregnancy-miscarriage-health-vending-machine-meals)

3. **Researchers at Edith Cowan University reported this month that a fiber blend combining resistant starch and psyllium husk improved gut bacteria in people with irritable bowel syndrome who were following a low-FODMAP diet, without worsening the bloating, pain, or other symptoms that diet is meant to control in the first place.** The trial was small, just 26 participants split between the fiber blend and a placebo, and the researchers are calling the results promising rather than settled. [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261112230450.htm)

## Tool of the Week: USDA's MyPlate Plan

![A laptop screen showing a simple dinner plate icon divided into food-group sections with a small target icon over it and a checkmark icon beside it, a government-building silhouette faint in the background, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261121_081439_720271e4-7e86-47d9-8ae9-91ac04fcd69f.png)

Since this issue is about a gap-checker that only checks two things, the pick this week is the free government tool that checks more of them for you by hand. USDA's MyPlate Plan asks for your age, sex, height, weight, and activity level, then hands back a personalized daily target broken out by food group: protein foods, fruits, vegetables, grains, and dairy, not just a calorie number. It won't flag a fiber gap the way the FoodNavigator piece says a GLP-1 user's diet needs to, but it's the clearest free way to see, group by group, whether what you actually ate today comes anywhere close to covering what you're supposed to. [USDA's MyPlate Plan](https://www.myplate.gov/myplate-plan)
