On November 1st, South Carolina became the latest state to start enforcing a federal waiver that tells SNAP recipients, at the checkout, which items their benefits can no longer buy. Most candy and sugar-sweetened soda, energy drinks included, are now off the list; diet and zero-sugar sodas, sports drinks, granola bars, and baked goods stayed on it. If a SNAP shopper's cart has a restricted item in it, the state's own guidance is blunt about what happens next: the register prompts for a different payment method for that item, no exceptions, no opt-out. South Carolina isn't alone. By USDA's own count, something like two dozen states now have an approved waiver doing some version of this, and Ohio, Kansas, Wyoming, and Nevada all picked up their own approvals earlier this year, each with its own start date and, more importantly, its own definition of what "candy" and "soda" actually mean. Kansas ties its definition to the state's own sales-tax code. South Carolina draws its own line between a sweetened drink and a diet one. None of these definitions match each other, and that's by design: the waiver program lets each state write its own list.

That's a genuinely hard classification problem, the same shape as the ultra-processed-food fight Issue 46 and Issue 48 both covered, except this one isn't sitting in a drawer at a federal budget office. It's live, today, at a cash register, deciding in real time whether a specific can of soda in a specific state counts as food. A shopping and tracking app is exactly the kind of thing that's supposed to help someone navigate a rule like that before they're standing at the register finding out the hard way. So I checked what Mr Bite knows about any of it.

Nothing. A repo-wide, case-insensitive search for "SNAP," "EBT," and "food stamp," across every file, every test, every migration, returns zero hits, for all three terms. Not a stray comment, not a dead code path, not a TODO. Whatever this program is, it has never once been named inside this codebase.

Assume that changes tomorrow and someone wants to build toward it. The first problem is more basic than any sugar-content rule: Mr Bite doesn't know what state a user lives in. `ProfileData`, the interface `calculateTargets.ts` builds every calorie and macro target from, carries weight, height, age, gender, activity level, and goal. No state field, no zip code, no address exists anywhere in the user's profile. Since every one of these waivers is state-specific, with different items and different start dates, there's no variable in the app today that could even select which rule applies to which person.

The second problem shows up in the two places "candy" and "soda" already exist in this codebase, for reasons that have nothing to do with SNAP. `datasetOptions.ts` lists "candy" as one of dozens of recipe categories pulled from a 50,000-recipe dataset, a tag for sorting recipes, not purchases. `categorizeIngredient` in `ingredientCategorizer.ts`, the function that sorts a shopping list into grocery aisles, matches "soda" with the same regex that catches juice, water, drink, beverage, coffee, and tea, and drops all of them into one bucket labeled "Pantry." Under that function, a can of regular Coke and a can of Diet Coke are identical: same regex, same bucket, no sugar content read, no distinction drawn anywhere. That's the exact line South Carolina's rule depends on, and this code was never built to see it.

The barcode path makes it worse in a way Issues 46 and 48 would recognize immediately. `normalizeOffProduct` in `openFoodFactsService.ts` already pulls a `category` and a `category_label` out of Open Food Facts' `categories_tags` field on every single scan, the one field that might actually carry something like "en:sodas" or "en:candies" straight from the source. Both get computed and attached to every `PackagedFood` the app returns. Then nobody reads them: a search across the two screens that call this function, `SearchFoodScreen` and `BarcodeScannerScreen`, turns up zero references to either field. The data arrives, gets parsed, and sits unused on every object the app builds, the identical pattern Issue 46 found with potassium and Issue 48 found with NOVA groups, just one layer further downstream.

None of this means Mr Bite should go build a point-of-sale eligibility checker. It means that when a real, live, state-by-state classification fight shows up in exactly the kind of data this app already touches on every scan, the app has no state to key off of, no distinction in its own shopping-list logic, and a category field it already computes and throws away.

1. **The FDA warned on October 26th that two lots of Darwin's Natural Pet Products' raw frozen Chicken Recipe for Cats tested positive for Salmonella, after a cat that ate the food was hospitalized, and said the company still hasn't recalled it.** The affected lots, 12731 and 12493, were made in July and April of 2026; the agency's advisory tells owners to throw the product out in a sealed container and disinfect anything it touched, since the firm has a history of similar findings going back to 2023. 2. **Vitality Works recalled one lot of 365 Whole Foods Market Ashwagandha Capsules on October 30th after routine sampling turned up Salmonella.** Lot 2025-26227, sold in 120-count bottles at Whole Foods stores nationwide and on Amazon between late 2025 and October 20th, 2026, is the only one affected; no illnesses have been reported, and refunds are available at any Whole Foods location. [Food Poisoning Bulletin](https://foodpoisoningbulletin.com/2026/365-whole-foods-ashwagandha-capsules-recalled-for-salmonella/)

## Tool of the Week: USDA's SNAP Food Restriction Waivers Page

![An open government database webpage icon branching into several small state-outline icons, connecting out to a simple shopping cart icon, warm cream background with coral and soft teal accents](https://d8j0ntlcm91z4.cloudfront.net/user_3ElLNwQpO0GqKZ9IN6sdAl7YP6i/hf_20261105_081619_2de0bf66-eb8d-415d-a7ba-c8189c68fb41.png)

Since this issue is about a rule that changes shape at every state line, the pick this week is the one free federal page that actually tracks all of it in one place: USDA's SNAP Food Restriction Waivers page, which lists every approved state waiver, what it restricts, when it takes effect, and which ones are tangled up in litigation. It won't tell you whether a specific can of soda in your specific state is still eligible today, store registers handle that, but it's the honest, verifiable starting point for "what does my state actually restrict," which beats guessing from a news headline about a different state entirely. [USDA SNAP Food Restriction Waivers](https://www.fns.usda.gov/snap/waivers/foodrestriction)
